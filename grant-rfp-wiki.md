## 1. Grant Types & Funder Archetypes

Understanding who the funder is shapes every decision downstream — vocabulary, tone, evidence standards, budget expectations, and section structure all shift based on funder type.

---

### Government Grants (Federal & Provincial)

**Who they are:** Public agencies disbursing taxpayer funds. In Canada: federal departments (Heritage, IRCC, NRC, PHAC), provincial ministries, Crown corporations. In the US: federal agencies (NIH, NEA, HUD, USDA), state programs.

**What they prioritize:** Accountability, measurable outcomes, policy alignment, broad community reach, equity and inclusion, value for money.

**Language register:** Formal, policy-aligned, technical. Use the funder's exact program terminology. Mirror language from the program guidelines back into the application.

**Evaluation approach:** Scored rubrics with explicit criteria and weights. Often has mandatory eligibility requirements that disqualify automatically if missed.

**Budget expectations:** Detailed line items, overhead/indirect cost rates (often capped — commonly 15–20%), multi-year projections for multi-year grants. May require matching funds or in-kind contributions.

**Common mistakes:** Using mission-driven emotional language in a policy-driven application. Ignoring eligibility requirements buried in schedules or appendices.

---

### Community & Private Foundations

**Who they are:** Charitable organizations that disburse investment income or donor contributions. Range from large (Gates, MacArthur, Rockefeller) to small family foundations.

**What they prioritize:** Strategic alignment with their funding pillars, org credibility, relationships, demonstrated community need, realistic theory of change.

**Language register:** Mission-driven, narrative, relational. Foundations often want to feel like partners, not auditors. Storytelling and community voice carry weight here.

**Evaluation approach:** Often less rigid than government — a program officer may read holistically rather than rubric-scoring. Relationships and prior funding history matter.

**Budget expectations:** Simpler than government grants. Project budgets rather than full org budgets. Indirect cost rates may be accepted without a cap if justified.

**Common mistakes:** Writing in bureaucratic language inappropriate for a relational funder. Failing to connect the work to their specific funding pillars.

---

### Corporate Giving Programs

**Who they are:** Corporate social responsibility (CSR) programs or corporate foundations. Examples: TD Friends of the Environment, RBC Foundation, Home Depot Foundation.

**What they prioritize:** Alignment with brand values and business interests, employee engagement opportunities, geographic proximity to operations, visibility and recognition.

**Language register:** Business-friendly, outcome-focused, impact-per-dollar. Acknowledge the partnership angle — corporate funders often want co-branding or volunteer engagement.

**Evaluation approach:** Varies widely. Some use formal RFP processes; others accept letters of inquiry or have open applications.

**Budget expectations:** Often smaller awards ($5K–$50K). Simple budgets. Recognition/acknowledgment line items are expected.

**Common mistakes:** Not mentioning recognition opportunities. Ignoring the geographic or sector alignment with their business.

---

### Cross-Cutting Rules

- Always use the funder's own vocabulary — do not substitute synonyms
- Never apply government-grant formality to a foundation application or vice versa
- If funder type is ambiguous, default to formal and flag for human review
- All claims must be truthful regardless of funder type — no exceptions

## 2. RFP Anatomy

A Request for Proposals (RFP) — also called a Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO), Call for Proposals (CFP), or Grant Guidelines — is the document that defines everything the application must include. Reading it correctly before writing a single word is the most important step.

---

### Standard RFP Sections

| Section | What to extract |
|---|---|
| **Program Overview** | Funder's priorities, theory of change, what they fund and don't fund |
| **Eligibility Requirements** | Org type, geography, registration status, prior funding restrictions — read carefully, flag every hard requirement |
| **Funding Details** | Award amount (min/max), project period, multi-year options, matching fund requirements |
| **Application Requirements** | Required sections, page/word limits per section, file format, font size, margin rules |
| **Evaluation Criteria** | Scoring rubric — criteria names, point weights (e.g. 30 pts / 100), what the reviewers are looking for |
| **Deadline & Submission** | Application deadline (date and time), submission portal or email, late policy |
| **Reporting Requirements** | Mid-term and final reports, financial reporting intervals, outcome metrics required |
| **Contact Information** | Program officer name and email for clarification questions |

---

### Evaluation Criteria — How to Read Them

When explicit criteria and weights are given:
- Record each criterion exactly as named by the funder
- Note the point weight (e.g. "Organizational Capacity — 20 points")
- Read the description of what earns full marks vs. partial marks
- These criteria become the scoring dimensions in the Funder-Org Alignment match report

When criteria are **not explicit**:
- Infer from the program description — what the funder says it values = implied criteria
- Flag all inferred criteria as `[INFERRED — not explicitly stated in RFP]`
- Do not present inferred criteria as confirmed

---

### Eligibility — Mandatory STOP Check

Before any narrative work begins, every eligibility requirement must be confirmed met:

- Legal status (registered charity, nonprofit, Indigenous organization, etc.)
- Geographic scope (must serve X region / be incorporated in X province)
- Organization size (annual revenue caps or minimums)
- Prior funding restrictions ("previous recipients ineligible" or "must not have received X in last 2 years")
- Project type restrictions ("capital projects ineligible" / "salaries not funded")

**If any eligibility requirement cannot be confirmed from the org profile → halt and ask before proceeding.**

---

### Word & Page Limits

- Treat limits as hard constraints, not targets
- Track word count per section throughout drafting
- Flag any section approaching 90% of the limit
- Flag any section that exceeds the limit as `[⚠ OVER LIMIT — must cut before submission]`

---

### Common RFP Traps

- Eligibility requirements buried in schedules, appendices, or footnotes — read the whole document
- Conflicting word limits between the body and an attached template — use the template if one exists
- "Up to X words" vs. "X words maximum" — both mean the same thing; do not exceed
- Submission deadlines in a different time zone than the applicant

## 3. Org Profile Components

The org profile is the AI's primary source of truth about the applicant organization. Everything in the grant application that describes the org must trace back to the org profile — if it isn't there, it must be flagged rather than inferred.

---

### Required Components

#### Mission & Vision
- The organization's stated purpose (mission)
- Long-term aspiration (vision, if distinct)
- Year founded, legal status (registered charity number, nonprofit incorporation)
- Geographic scope of operations (local, regional, national)

#### Programs & Services
- Names and descriptions of active programs
- Target population served per program
- Geographic reach per program
- Program age / years in operation
- Delivery model (direct service, capacity building, advocacy, etc.)

#### Track Record & Past Grants
- Previous grants received: funder name, amount, year, purpose
- Outcomes achieved (quantitative where available: people served, units delivered)
- Any prior relationship with the current funder (repeat applicant, prior grant, declined application)

#### Key Staff & Organizational Capacity
- Executive Director / CEO name and relevant experience
- Program lead(s) for the proposed project
- Board composition (size, relevant expertise)
- Number of staff (FTE and part-time) and volunteers

#### Financials Summary
- Most recent fiscal year: total revenue, total expenses, surplus/deficit
- Revenue breakdown: government grants %, foundation grants %, earned revenue %, donations %
- Current year budget (if available)
- Audited statements available (yes/no) and most recent audit year
- **Data currency field:** "Financials current as of: ___" — if blank, flag every financial figure `[HUMAN: confirm this is current]`

---

### What the AI Must Never Do with Org Profile Data

- Never invent statistics, program outcomes, or financial figures not present in the profile
- Never upgrade vague claims ("we serve many people" → "we serve 500 people annually") without a source
- Never assume a program exists based on the org's mission — only use explicitly stated programs
- If a required piece of information is missing → insert `[HUMAN: provide — not found in org profile]` and continue

---

### Profile Data Currency

Org profiles can go stale. The AI should check:
- Are financials from the most recent completed fiscal year?
- Are program descriptions current (not archived programs)?
- Are staff names and roles current?

If the profile does not include a "current as of" date → flag at the top of the match report: `[HUMAN: confirm org profile data is current before proceeding]`

## 4. Narrative Section Frameworks

Grant narratives are structured arguments. Each section has a job to do. Understanding what each section must accomplish — not just what it must contain — is what separates a funded application from a rejected one.

---

### Problem / Need Statement

**Job:** Convince the funder that the problem is real, significant, and that the org is positioned to address it.

**Must include:**
- The specific problem or gap being addressed
- Who is affected and how many (quantified where possible)
- Why the problem exists / root causes
- Geographic specificity — this problem, in this place
- Evidence: statistics, research, community data, lived experience
- Connection to the funder's stated priorities

**Common failures:**
- Describing the solution in the needs section (save it for project approach)
- Using national statistics to argue a local problem without localizing the data
- Vague claims without evidence: "Many people in our community struggle with X"

---

### Project Approach

**Job:** Describe exactly what will be done, by whom, when, and why this approach works.

**Must include:**
- Activities and deliverables (specific and concrete)
- Timeline or project phases
- Staff / partner roles
- Theory of change: why this approach addresses the need described
- Evidence base for the approach (research, best practices, prior org experience)
- How the work is distinct from existing services (avoid duplication)

**Common failures:**
- Activities listed without explanation of *why* they work
- Timelines that are vague ("Year 1: implement program" is not a timeline)
- Claiming an evidence-based model without citing the evidence

---

### Outcomes & Evaluation Plan

**Job:** Commit to specific, measurable results and explain how they will be tracked.

**Must include:**
- Outputs: what will be produced (workshops delivered, people trained, reports published)
- Outcomes: what will change as a result (short-term, medium-term)
- Indicators: how each outcome will be measured
- Data collection methods: surveys, intake forms, administrative data, third-party evaluation
- Reporting plan: when and how the funder will receive results

**Common failures:**
- Confusing outputs (activities) with outcomes (changes)
- Outcomes that are not measurable: "participants will feel empowered"
- No plan for how data will actually be collected

**Flag rule:** Any outcome number (e.g. "85% of participants will report improved skills") must be traced to org history or flagged `[HUMAN: verify this target is achievable based on past performance]`

---

### Organizational Qualifications

**Job:** Prove the org can deliver what it promised.

**Must include:**
- Relevant experience: past projects similar in scope and population
- Key staff credentials and relevant experience
- Partners and their roles (with evidence of confirmed partnerships)
- Org infrastructure: systems, facilities, technology in place
- Financial management capacity: audit status, financial controls

**Common failures:**
- Listing staff credentials irrelevant to the proposed project
- Claiming a partnership without evidence it is confirmed
- Describing capacity in general terms without tying it to the specific project

---

### Budget Narrative

See [Budget Conventions](#6-budget-conventions) for full guidance. In the narrative context:
- Every line item must be justified in plain language
- Dollar figures in the narrative must exactly match the budget spreadsheet
- Explain *why* each cost is necessary, not just what it is

---

### Section Sequencing

Always assemble sections in the order the RFP specifies — not in the logical writing order. The funder's required sequence takes priority over any internal logic.

## 5. Evidence & Citation Standards

Grant reviewers are skeptical readers. Every claim of need, impact, or effectiveness must be backed by evidence appropriate to the claim being made. The strength and type of evidence used signals the org's credibility.

---

### Evidence Source Hierarchy

#### Tier 1 — Strongest
- **Peer-reviewed academic research** — Published studies supporting the program model or establishing need. Cite author, year, journal.
- **Government statistics & reports** — Statistics Canada, Census Bureau, federal/provincial ministry data, health agency surveillance data. Official and credible for needs statements.
- **Sector-specific databases** — CMHC housing data, CIHI health data, Crime Severity Index, school board data. Highly relevant when matching grant vertical.

#### Tier 2 — Strong
- **Org's own outcome data** — Program evaluations, annual reports, intake/exit surveys, administrative records. Strongest for demonstrating the org's track record. Must be current and verifiable.
- **Needs assessments** — Community needs assessments conducted by the org, a municipality, or a recognized research body.
- **Government program evaluations** — Published evaluations of similar publicly funded programs.

#### Tier 3 — Supportive
- **Sector reports & white papers** — Reports from recognized sector bodies (United Way, Community Foundations of Canada, sector-specific associations). Good for context.
- **Local media & community reports** — Useful for illustrating local conditions, not for statistical claims.
- **Client testimonials** — Powerful for narrative impact and lived experience. Not a substitute for quantitative evidence but adds essential human context.

---

### How to Use Each Source Type

| Claim type | Appropriate source |
|---|---|
| "This problem exists and is significant" | Government stats, sector databases, academic research |
| "This population is underserved" | Census data, needs assessments, local government data |
| "This approach works" | Academic research, government program evaluations, org outcome data |
| "Our org has done this before" | Org's own outcome data, past grant reports |
| "The community wants this" | Needs assessment, testimonials, consultation records |
| "The problem affects X number of people" | Statistics Canada, CIHI, Census — cite year and geography |

---

### Citation Format

- Government/academic: Author or Agency (Year). *Title*. Source URL if available.
- Org data: "According to [Org Name]'s 2023–24 Annual Report..."
- Testimonials: First name only or "a program participant" — never full names without consent
- All statistics must include the year they were collected — outdated stats weaken credibility

---

### What the AI Must Not Do

- **Never fabricate a statistic** — if a claim needs a number and none is available, write `[HUMAN: insert local statistic here — e.g. from Statistics Canada or municipal report]`
- **Never cite a source it cannot verify** — do not invent journal titles, report names, or URLs
- **Never present Tier 3 evidence as Tier 1** — a news article is not equivalent to a peer-reviewed study
- **Flag all statistics with year and geography** — a 2015 national statistic is not evidence of a 2025 local condition

---

### Localization Rule

National or provincial statistics must always be connected to the local context. If only national data is available, acknowledge it: "While national data shows X, local conditions in [region] suggest..." and flag for the human to add local evidence if available.

## 6. Budget Conventions

The budget is not a formality — reviewers use it to assess the org's financial management capacity and the credibility of the proposed project. A budget that doesn't add up, or a narrative that contradicts the numbers, is a significant red flag.

---

### Standard Budget Structure

#### Personnel Costs
- Staff names or titles, % of time allocated to the project, annual salary, calculated project cost
- Format: `Program Coordinator — 0.5 FTE @ $55,000/year = $27,500`
- Include benefits as a separate line (typically 15–25% of salary)
- Distinguish between existing staff (paid from grant) and new hires

#### Consultant & Contract Services
- Name or role, rate (hourly or daily), estimated hours/days, total
- Must be justified: why a contractor rather than staff?

#### Program/Direct Costs
- Materials, supplies, participant costs, food, transportation
- Itemize anything over $500; group small items into categories

#### Equipment
- Items over a threshold (often $1,000–$5,000) are capital and may require special approval or be ineligible
- Check RFP for capital expenditure rules

#### Travel & Accommodation
- Per-kilometer rates (use government rates), airfare estimates, hotel per diems
- Justify why travel is necessary

#### Overhead / Indirect Costs
- Also called administrative costs, G&A, or facilities & administration (F&A)
- Many funders cap this: government grants often 15–20%, foundations vary (0–25%)
- Calculate as a % of direct costs or total budget — use whichever the funder specifies
- **Always check the RFP for the cap before calculating**

---

### Budget Narrative Rules

- Every line item must have a one-to-two sentence justification explaining *why* it is needed for this project
- Dollar figures in the narrative must **exactly match** the budget spreadsheet — no rounding, no approximation
- After generating the budget narrative, produce a reconciliation table: every dollar figure mentioned in the narrative alongside its source line item
- If a figure in the org profile contradicts the budget → flag for human review before proceeding

---

### Matching Funds & In-Kind

- Some grants require matching contributions (cash or in-kind) from the applicant or other sources
- In-kind contributions (volunteer time, donated space, equipment) must be valued at fair market rates
- Volunteer time: use sector standard rates (check Imagine Canada or Independent Sector for current rates)
- Confirm whether in-kind counts toward the match requirement — not all funders accept it

---

### Common Budget Errors

| Error | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Narrative says "$45,000 for staff" but budget shows $47,500 | Reviewer catches the discrepancy — signals poor attention to detail |
| Overhead exceeds the funder's cap | Application may be disqualified or budget returned for revision |
| No benefits line for staff costs | Unrealistic budget — reviewer assumes org doesn't understand true costs |
| Capital items not flagged | May be ineligible — always check RFP |
| Budget doesn't balance (revenue ≠ expenses) | Automatic red flag for financial management capacity |

---

### AI Budget Rules

- Never calculate salary figures without the org-provided rate — flag `[HUMAN: provide salary rate for this position]`
- Never assume a benefits rate — use org-provided rate or flag
- Always flag if overhead calculation exceeds 20% — check RFP for the cap
- Always produce the reconciliation table in Sub-build 3

## 7. Funder-Org Alignment Methodology

Alignment scoring is the core analytical step of the Grant Response Generator. It determines which parts of the org profile to lead with, where the narrative is strong, and where gaps need to be addressed or reframed.

---

### The Alignment Process

#### Step 1 — Extract funder priorities
From the RFP, identify:
- The funder's stated mission and funding pillars
- The program's specific goals and intended outcomes
- Explicit evaluation criteria (if listed with weights)
- Implicit priorities (language used repeatedly, what the overview section emphasizes)

#### Step 2 — Extract org capabilities
From the org profile, identify:
- Active programs and their populations served
- Geographic footprint
- Track record: past grants, outcomes achieved
- Staff expertise
- Partnerships
- Financial stability indicators

#### Step 3 — Score alignment

For each evaluation criterion (explicit or inferred):

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **5** | Strong match — org has direct experience, evidence, and outcomes in this exact area |
| **4** | Good match — org has relevant experience with minor gaps |
| **3** | Partial match — some connection but requires reframing or additional context |
| **2** | Weak match — limited connection, significant gap to address |
| **1** | Poor match — little to no relevant evidence in org profile |

For each score, provide:
- A one-line justification citing specific evidence from the org profile
- If score is 3 or below: a note on how to address or reframe the gap

#### Step 4 — Identify differentiators
The top 2–3 areas where the org scores highest AND the funder weights most heavily = the differentiators to lead with in the narrative.

#### Step 5 — Flag gaps
Any criterion scoring 2 or below = a gap. For each gap:
- Suggest a reframe strategy (e.g. adjacent experience, partnership, commitment to build capacity)
- Flag `[HUMAN: review this gap — consider whether to address directly or reframe]`

---

### Inferred Criteria Handling

When the RFP does not list explicit criteria:
1. Derive criteria from the program description and funder priorities
2. Label the entire criteria table `[INFERRED — not explicitly stated in RFP]`
3. Require human confirmation before using the inferred criteria to drive the narrative

---

### Output Format — Match Report

```
FUNDER SUMMARY
Name: [Funder name]
Deadline: [Date]
Award: [Amount range]
Eligibility confirmed: [Yes / Pending — see flags]

REQUIRED SECTIONS
[Section name] — [word/page limit]
...

ALIGNMENT SCORES
Criterion | Weight | Score | Evidence
...

TOP 3 DIFFERENTIATORS
1. [Criterion] — [Why the org leads here]
2.
3.

GAPS TO ADDRESS
1. [Criterion] — [Score] — [Reframe strategy]
...
```

---

### What the Match Report Feeds

The match report is the input to Sub-build 2. Every narrative section should be written with the differentiators leading and the gaps addressed using the reframe strategies. Writers should not start Sub-build 2 without the match report in hand.

## 8. Context & Vocabulary Switching

Grant writing language is not one-size-fits-all. The same program described to a federal government funder, a community foundation, and a healthcare corporate giving program requires three different registers, vocabularies, and emphasis patterns. Getting this wrong is immediately obvious to an experienced reviewer.

---

### Register by Funder Type

| Funder Type | Register | Tone | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal government | Formal, policy-aligned, technical | Objective, measured | Accountability, reach, value for money, policy outcomes |
| Provincial government | Formal, program-aligned | Objective, service-focused | Service delivery, regional equity, measurable targets |
| Community foundation | Mission-driven, narrative | Warm, relational | Community impact, lived experience, partnership, sustainability |
| Corporate giving | Business-friendly, outcome-focused | Professional, partnership-oriented | Brand alignment, employee engagement, impact per dollar |

---

### Register by Org Vertical

#### Healthcare & Social Services
- Use clinical and social determinants language where appropriate
- Acceptable terms: evidence-based, trauma-informed, wraparound services, continuum of care, health equity
- Avoid: vague wellness language without clinical grounding
- Evidence standard: high — peer-reviewed research and epidemiological data expected

#### Arts & Culture
- Emphasize community access, cultural expression, audience reach, artist development
- Acceptable terms: cultural equity, artistic excellence, community-engaged practice
- Avoid: purely economic justifications unless the funder explicitly values them
- Evidence standard: moderate — attendance data, reach, participant experience

#### Education & Youth
- Emphasize outcomes for young people, school/community partnerships, evidence-based curriculum
- Acceptable terms: at-risk youth, wraparound supports, academic achievement, social-emotional learning (SEL)
- Avoid: deficit framing of youth — use strength-based language
- Evidence standard: high for outcomes claims; school board / Ministry data useful

#### Environment & Conservation
- Emphasize measurable environmental outcomes, science-based targets, community stewardship
- Acceptable terms: ecosystem services, carbon sequestration, biodiversity, land stewardship
- Avoid: vague "green" language without measurable outcomes
- Evidence standard: scientific data, baseline and target measurements

#### Housing & Homelessness
- Emphasize Housing First principles, harm reduction, systems navigation
- Acceptable terms: unhoused individuals, people with lived experience, Housing First, by-name list
- Avoid: outdated stigmatizing terms ("homeless people" — use "people experiencing homelessness")
- Evidence standard: CMHC data, point-in-time counts, by-name list data

#### Indigenous Organizations
- Centre Indigenous self-determination, cultural reclamation, community-led design
- Use Nation or community names specifically — never generic "Indigenous community"
- Incorporate UNDRIP language where relevant to federal applications
- Avoid: colonial framing, deficit narratives, speaking for communities without their voice
- Evidence standard: community-defined — org's own data and community voice carry significant weight

---

### Vocabulary Matching Rule

Always use the funder's exact vocabulary from the RFP. If the RFP says "marginalized populations," use that phrase — do not substitute "vulnerable communities" or "equity-deserving groups" unless the funder uses those terms.

**When in doubt: mirror the RFP's language back into the application.**

---

### Switching Checklist

Before drafting, confirm:
- [ ] Funder type identified (government / foundation / corporate)
- [ ] Org vertical identified
- [ ] RFP vocabulary list extracted (key terms used ≥ 2 times in the RFP)
- [ ] Register set for this application
- [ ] Forbidden terms / outdated language identified for this vertical

## 9. Quality Rules & Hard Limits

These rules apply to every output, every section, every grant type, every org vertical — without exception. They exist to protect the applying organization from submissions that could damage their credibility, misrepresent their work, or result in funded commitments they cannot meet.

---

### Hard Rules — Never Violate

#### 1. Be truthful always
Every claim in a grant application must be traceable to a verifiable source — the org profile, the RFP, a cited document, or the user's explicit input. If a claim cannot be verified, it must be flagged. It must never be invented to fill a gap.

#### 2. Ask when information is missing
If information needed to complete a section is not present in the inputs:
- Insert `[HUMAN: provide — [specific information needed]]`
- Continue drafting the rest of the section around the gap
- Do not invent plausible-sounding information to fill it

#### 3. Never fabricate statistics
Statistics, percentages, counts, and outcome numbers must come from a cited source. If no source is available:
- Insert `[HUMAN: insert statistic here — suggested source: Statistics Canada / CIHI / municipal report]`
- Never write a specific number without a traceable source

#### 4. Never overpromise outcomes
Outcome targets must be grounded in the org's historical performance or explicitly set by the user. Do not write outcome targets that sound good but exceed what the org has demonstrated capacity to deliver. Flag any target not traceable to past performance: `[HUMAN: verify this target is achievable based on past program results]`

#### 5. Never misrepresent organizational capacity
Do not describe staff, systems, partnerships, or infrastructure that is not confirmed in the org profile. If a partnership is mentioned but not confirmed, flag it: `[HUMAN: confirm this partnership is secured before submission]`

#### 6. Never use the funder's jargon incorrectly
Using a funder's terminology in a way that contradicts their definition is worse than not using it at all. If unsure of a term's meaning in the funder's context, use plain language instead and flag for human review.

#### 7. Budget figures must reconcile
Every dollar figure in the narrative must match the budget spreadsheet exactly. No rounding. No approximations. See [Budget Conventions](#6-budget-conventions).

---

### Flag Taxonomy

| Flag | When to use |
|---|---|
| `[HUMAN: verify this claim]` | Claim is plausible but not directly sourced from org profile or cited document |
| `[HUMAN: provide — X]` | Information needed but not available in any input |
| `[HUMAN: confirm this is current]` | Data present in org profile but currency is unknown |
| `[HUMAN: confirm this partnership is secured]` | Partnership mentioned but not confirmed in org profile |
| `[HUMAN: verify this target is achievable]` | Outcome target not traceable to past performance |
| `[INFERRED — not stated in RFP]` | Evaluation criteria derived by the AI, not explicitly listed |
| `[⚠ OVER WORD LIMIT]` | Section exceeds RFP word limit |

---

### Clarification Trigger Conditions

The AI must stop and ask the user before proceeding when:
- Eligibility requirements cannot be confirmed from the org profile
- The RFP appears to be image-based and no text content was extracted
- The org profile contains no financial data
- The proposed project is not described anywhere in the org profile
- The RFP contains contradictory instructions (e.g. two different word limits for the same section)

---

### Flag Count Reporting

At the top of every narrative document output, include:
```
⚑ This draft contains [N] flagged items requiring human review before submission.
   Review all flags before finalizing.
```

This prevents flag fatigue by making the total visible upfront.

**Related:** Applies to all articles — this is the root constraint layer for the entire wiki.

## 10. Output Formats & Submission Standards

The best-written grant application fails if it is submitted in the wrong format, with sections in the wrong order, or missing required attachments. This article covers how to structure and format outputs for submission.

---

### Document Structure Rule

Always assemble the final document in **the order the RFP specifies** — not the order the sections were drafted. After assembly, output a **section order checklist**:

```
Section Order Verification
[ ] 1. Executive Summary — drafted ✓ — word count: 247/250
[ ] 2. Organizational Background — drafted ✓ — word count: 498/500
[ ] 3. Project Description — drafted ✓ — word count: 892/1000
[ ] 4. Evaluation Plan — drafted ✓ — word count: 445/500
[ ] 5. Budget — attached ✓
Human: verify section order matches RFP before submitting
```

---

### Format by Submission Type

#### Online Portal Submission
- Content is pasted into form fields — each field has its own character or word limit
- **Character limits ≠ word limits** — check which unit the portal uses
- Some portals strip formatting (bold, bullets) — write for plain text
- Paste into a plain text editor first to strip formatting, then into the portal
- Save progress frequently — portals time out

#### Document Upload (PDF or Word)
- Follow specified font, size, and margin rules exactly
- Embed all required sections in one document unless the RFP specifies separate files
- File naming convention: use the funder's specified convention if given; otherwise: `[OrgName]_[ProgramName]_[Year].pdf`
- Convert to PDF unless Word is specifically required

#### Email Submission
- Subject line: use the exact format specified in the RFP
- Attach all required documents; confirm attachment names match requirements
- Send from the contact email specified in the application

---

### Required Attachments Checklist

Common attachments (varies by funder — always check the RFP):

| Attachment | Notes |
|---|---|
| Registered charity / nonprofit certificate | Current and valid |
| Most recent audited financial statements | Check if prior year or current year required |
| Current year operating budget | Full org budget, not just project budget |
| Board member list | Names, roles, sometimes affiliations |
| Organizational chart | Staff structure |
| Letters of support / partnership letters | On partner letterhead, signed, dated within 6 months |
| Logic model or theory of change diagram | Some funders require a visual |
| Proof of Indigenous status / charitable registration | For specific program streams |

---

### Word & Character Count Standards

- **Word count:** Count using the word processor's built-in tool — not estimated
- **Character count:** Includes spaces unless the RFP specifies "characters without spaces"
- **Page limits:** At specified font and margins — do not reduce font size to fit more content
- **Appendices:** Check if they count toward the page limit — many funders exclude appendices but reviewers may not read them

---

### Final Pre-Submission Checklist

```
[ ] All required sections present and in RFP order
[ ] All word/page limits met (no section over limit)
[ ] All [HUMAN: verify] flags resolved
[ ] Budget narrative figures match budget spreadsheet exactly
[ ] All required attachments included with correct filenames
[ ] Eligibility requirements confirmed
[ ] Submission deadline confirmed (date, time, time zone)
[ ] Submission method confirmed (portal / email / courier)
[ ] Confirmation receipt requested (email submission) or portal confirmation screenshot saved
```
